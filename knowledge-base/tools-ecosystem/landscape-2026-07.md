## Append: 2026-07-13 landscape monitor update

*Added by the 2026-07-13 monitor run (window: 2026-07-06 → 2026-07-13). No flagship platform launch this cycle. The week's vendor news was a thin single-vendor roundup. The one skill change is a version bump — `sales/agentic-retail-media-mediation` v1.0 → v1.1 — driven by a **regulatory** signal the prior cycle did not catch plus two independent consumer-research datasets that quantify the same risk.*

**§1 FTC proposed policy statement on the suppression of accuracy in AI systems — July 1, 2026 (ACT — version bump `agentic-retail-media-mediation` v1.1).** The Commission issued for public comment a proposed policy statement advancing the theory that an AI company which bends its system's outputs away from what users would reasonably expect — objective, accurate answers — may be deceiving them under **Section 5 of the FTC Act**. The deception reasoning runs through the *representation*: a company that markets a system as fit for a task, and then quietly tunes it to serve an undisclosed goal, has made a promise its product does not keep. Comments close **July 31, 2026**; the authorizing vote was 2-0. The statement also asserts that a state AI law (it names **Colorado's Artificial Intelligence Act**) is "impliedly preempted to the extent it conflicts with a federal regulatory scheme," and it follows a December executive order directing the Commission to take up state laws that would force alteration of what the FTC terms the "truthful outputs of AI models."

Two things matter for retail, and both are *read-across*, not direct application — the statement is aimed principally at AI developers and at ideologically-motivated distortion, and it is a proposal rather than a final rule. (i) **Output-steering doctrine.** A retailer whose shopping assistant is marketed as objective, unbiased, or best-option-finding, while retail-media revenue silently shapes what it recommends, sits squarely in the conceptual zone the statement describes. This is the merchant-actionable core: the exposure is not the *presence* of sponsorship (which disclosure handles) but an **unsubstantiated objectivity claim** sitting on top of a bid-influenced ranker. (ii) **Preemption instability.** A merchant that hard-codes a flat state-by-state disclosure patchwork is building on ground the federal government is actively contesting; the durable architecture is a federal floor plus versioned per-state overlays. Both are now sub-checks in `agentic-retail-media-mediation` step 14.

**§2 Paid-influence trust research — two independent datasets (ACT — same version bump, calibration inputs).** **Quad / The Harris Poll, "The New Rules of Retail Trust in the Age of AI"** (released April 13, 2026; fielded Feb 5–7, 2026; n = 2,180 US adults): roughly **75%** say they would place less trust in an AI shopping agent whose recommendations they believed advertisers had paid to shape — and the same share say they would place less trust in the **advertiser that paid**, so the reputational cost is borne by the demand side as well as by the surface. Related: only ~39% trust AI agents to make everyday purchases for them; 73% say algorithm-driven pricing makes it hard to know they are getting the best deal. **ACI Worldwide / YouGov** (released June 28–29, 2026; fielded June 19–22, 2026; n = 2,080 UK adults): roughly **60%** would stop using an AI shopping agent after a *single* mistake; only ~19% trust AI assistants for everyday purchasing decisions against ~55% who trust a human adviser; ~69% do not trust AI even when it follows rules they themselves set.

Together these convert "sponsored placement erodes trust" from an intuition into a budgetable constraint. The mediation skill previously treated shopper-trust signals as an *amber* CSAT footnote in the yield loop; v1.1 promotes paid-influence perception to a **red-line** metric, adds a `retail_media.max_sponsored_load` ceiling (sponsored load as a trust-consuming resource with a budget, not a free yield lever), and uses the one-mistake abandonment finding as the explicit justification for the aggressive same-day rollback windows the skill already specified. Note the direction-of-effect nuance worth carrying: the Quad data also finds ~45% of Americans (and majorities of Gen Z / Millennials) place *more* faith in AI tools than in-store associates for unbiased recommendations — so the trust being risked is real and currently favorable, which is precisely why it is worth protecting.

## Append: 2026-07-20 landscape monitor update

*Added by the 2026-07-20 monitor run (window: 2026-07-13 → 2026-07-20). No flagship platform GA this cycle. The week's vendor news was another single-vendor roundup. The one skill change is a restrained version bump — `operations/return-fraud-image-shield` v1.2 → v1.3 — driven by a well-corroborated **threat-surface** signal: AI-fabricated return evidence has expanded from the damage photo to the whole claim bundle, and independent trade reporting now names merchants who have caught it.*

**§1 AI-synthetic return evidence broadens from the photo to the full claim bundle (ACT: `return-fraud-image-shield` v1.2 → v1.3).** The skill already treated AI-generated *damage photos* as a scored signal (added off the 2026-06-01 monitor). What is new this cycle, and now multi-source, is that generative AI is being used to fabricate the *rest* of the claim, cheaply and at scale: crushed-package and shipping-label images, screenshots of "carrier delivery" records, forwarded "customer-service approval" screenshots, and complaint narratives written to match a specific merchant's return policy — and the same fabricated bundle is submitted to several merchants at once. A Practical Ecommerce analysis (July 16, 2026, Armando Roggio) laid out the artifact list and the "scalable deception across transaction, dispute, logistics, and communication stages" framing; Modern Retail reported named incidents — Boll & Branch's CEO caught an **AI generator watermark** on a "torn sheets" photo, and Bogg has built standardized handling language for manipulated-image claims; PYMNTS and Chief Marketer corroborated independently. Prevalence indicators: network fraud-prevention vendor **Yofi** describes AI return scams moving from isolated incidents to daily events across thousands of merchant accounts; deepfake-detection firm **Pindrop** estimates roughly three in ten retail fraud *attempts* are now AI-generated. Scale anchor: the NRF / Happy Returns *2025 Retail Returns Landscape* put U.S. returns near **$850B** with about **9%** estimated fraudulent and an online return rate near **19–20%** (well above stores). A June 2026 academic study (arXiv 2606.03215) documents the same pattern in China.

**Relevance 8 | Novelty 7.** Relevance is high because the signal maps directly onto an existing skill and is corroborated by named-merchant incidents plus primary industry data rather than a single vendor's launch. Novelty is moderate because the *image* case was already covered; what v1.3 adds is genuinely new to the skill: (i) **signal-a broadened from image-only to submitted-evidence authenticity** — every non-photo artifact the merchant accepts (carrier screenshot, prior-approval screenshot, shipping label, complaint text) is now screened, under the governing rule *verify against the merchant's own system of record, never trust the customer's screenshot when you hold the authoritative version of the same fact*; (ii) a **generator-watermark / provenance-tag scan** added alongside C2PA (the Boll & Branch tell); (iii) a **cross-merchant reuse** sub-signal under a new `network_fraud_signal.vendor` config key (single-merchant views cannot see reuse; network vendors can); (iv) a **retro-audit sweep** of the trailing 30–90 days of already-approved no-return-required refunds — the fastest payback for a capability that "exploded overnight," and the cleanest way to *measure* the AI-synthetic share for calibration rather than guess it, with an explicit no-automatic-clawback guardrail; and (v) a **step-1 calibration refresh** that treats the AI-synthetic slice as a rising input re-fit from the merchant's own trend, anchored to the ~$850B / 9% / ~19–20%-online figures. Two config keys added (`submitted_evidence.accepted_artifacts`, `network_fraud_signal.vendor`); config-utilization checklist grew nine → eleven; a retro-audit-sweep output requirement was added. Restraint: this is a version bump, not a new skill — it is a threat-surface expansion of an existing defensive program, not a new workflow.

## Append: 2026-07-28 landscape monitor update

*Added by the 2026-07-28 monitor run (window: 2026-07-20 → 2026-07-28). No flagship platform GA this cycle. The signal that clears the action bar is **regulatory, not vendor**: the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations became enforceable on 2 August 2026 — days after this run — and one of the 24 skills had zero AI-disclosure content despite being the single most common "system that talks to a customer" surface in the library.*

**§1 EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations enforceable 2 August 2026 (ACT: `customer-service-reply` v3.3 → v3.4).** Article 50(1)'s chatbot-disclosure duty — a system "intended to interact directly with natural persons" must make clear the person is dealing with a machine, unless obvious from context — becomes enforceable EU-wide on 2 August 2026, alongside the Act's full penalty regime (up to €35M or 7% of global annual turnover). Corroborated independently by the European Commission's own FAQ on Article 50, Greenberg Traurig, and multiple compliance-law trackers (Bratby Law, aiactblog.nl, artificialintelligenceact.eu, Technology.org), plus Forbes trade coverage (July 19) on US watchdogs (FTC, Sen. Warner's proposed AI Agent Act) taking a parallel interest in AI-mediated retail interactions. Three of the library's 24 skills (`agentic-retail-media-mediation`, `clienteling-program-design`, `personalization-strategy`) already cite "EU AI Act Article 50" in their regulations reference lists, but `customer-service-reply` — the skill that drafts the actual reply text for email, live chat, social, and marketplace messages — had **no AI-disclosure content at all**. That is the gap this cycle closes.

**Relevance 8 | Novelty 7.** Relevance is high: this is a primary-source, dated, binding compliance deadline (not a proposal) landing within days of the run, with direct exposure for any merchant running a live chat channel as an unattended chatbot. Novelty is moderate-to-high: the *citation* of Article 50 already existed in three skills, but the concrete enforcement date, the specific two-part disclosure mechanic (first-message statement + persistent session indicator), the distinction from the separate Article 50(2) synthetic-media-marking grace period (2 December 2026, which does not apply to text replies), and — most importantly — extending the disclosure gate to a skill that had zero prior coverage, are all new. Deduped against all prior monitor logs (grep for "AI Act," "Article 50," "chatbot disclosure" across `monitor-log/` turns up only generic jurisdiction-matrix mentions from April–May, never this skill or this deadline) and against the skill text itself.

**Action:** `customer-service-reply` v3.3 → v3.4. New **step 9 "AI-disclosure gate"**: mirrors the human-review-vs-unattended-send distinction `clienteling-program-design` already uses (disclosure not required when a named agent reviews and sends; required when an automated / unattended system releases the reply without review — most commonly a live-chat channel run as a bot, or an after-hours auto-responder on any channel). Config-utilization checklist renumbered to step 10. Added an **AI-disclosure flag** to output requirements, a regulations reference to `knowledge-base/regulations/` (Article 50, California AI Transparency Act, Utah AI Policy Act — matching the citation set already used in `clienteling-program-design`), a note in Required Input item 2 to capture human-reviewed-vs-unattended per channel, and a worked-example line showing the flag resolving to "not required" for the existing reference run (a human-reviewed email). Nothing removed; purely additive.

**Restraint check:** targeted a single skill with the clearest, most acute gap rather than editing all three skills that already carry the citation — those three already structurally handle the human-review-vs-automation distinction (or, in `personalization-strategy`'s case, cite the Act only for high-risk profiling categories, a different obligation) and did not need a change this cycle. `agentic-retail-media-mediation`'s existing v1.1 FTC/state-preemption gate is a related but distinct obligation (deceptive-recommendation risk vs. disclosure-of-machine-interaction) and is left as-is.

Relevance 6, novelty 6 — single-vendor acquisition, no second named vendor doing camera-verified real-time shelf-to-catalog reconciliation yet, so no gate met. **New pipeline candidate:** a "camera-verified on-shelf inventory accuracy" sub-check or input source for `inventory-reorder-brief` / `in-store-retail-media-inventory`, triggered by a second named vendor (marketplace, grocer, or platform) shipping the same real-time video-to-inventory reconciliation pattern.
